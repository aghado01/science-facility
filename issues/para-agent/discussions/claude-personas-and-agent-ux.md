Worth distinguishing the two mechanisms you've seen me use, because they're different qualities of signal and the built-in should model the better one. What I set manually with the scheduler is the weak form — a timer that guesses when to check back, which is just polling wearing a nicer coat; pick the interval wrong and you either wake early for nothing or leave a finished turn sitting idle. The strong form is what the harness does with background tasks: the work itself fires the notification when it completes, no interval guessing at all. Para-agent is perfectly positioned for the strong form, because it — unlike me watching a pane — actually knows the moment the headless process exits. So "arming" ideally means: exchange tool returns the handle, and completion itself pushes the signal, with a timer only as the fallback heartbeat for hung turns — which is precisely the shape of the L4 fix. The completion-signaling primitive from the friction list and this arming feature are one piece of infrastructure seen from two sides: the MCP detecting completion is the mechanism, the armed signal is its delivery.

One delivery-side note: the signal has to land in the primary's harness, not just exist in para-agent's state. When the primary is a Claude Code session, the native hook is the task-notification path — a wait-on-handle tool the harness tracks as background work gets the strong-form push for free. A file-sentinel or journal flag works as the transport-agnostic fallback for primaries whose harness can't be pushed to. Designing arming as "para-agent knows completion; delivery adapts to the driver's harness" keeps the feature portable across whatever sits in the primary seat later — including the local-gguf workers, whose harness will be yours to define anyway.

# well i think that non-gguf agents like agy, claude and codex all probably expose a similar level of resume semantics that essentially boil down to pointing to a session identifier of some sort to pull that session and its context. you're right about GGUFs and in that case it would point to a different abstraction in my opinion, one that could be coordinated with the other kind, but by their nature gguf models don't have this native notion, which is an important distinction pointing to two separate resume concepts and i would probably name them differently, reserving one notion for the native resume that most frontier models would expose

That's a cleaner cut than my capability-tier framing — not one axis with grades, but two genuinely different concepts that deserve different names. And the deep difference is ownership of state: in the native kind, the client holds the session and the backend holds only a pointer into someone else's store; in the gguf kind, there is no someone else — the backend's own journal _is_ the session record, and continuity is something para-agent reconstructs rather than requests. Naming them separately keeps the abstraction from ever quietly promising equivalence, because they fail differently too. The pointer kind fails by dangling — the client's store expired or moved, and the backend can't repair that, only detect it. The reconstruction kind can't dangle, since the backend owns the material, but it fails by fidelity: a rebuilt context is a _choice_ about what to rehydrate — the visible reply chain certainly, but thinking traces and tool results are exactly the streams your progressive-disclosure design keeps separate, so what gets replayed becomes a deliberate knob rather than an implicit given. Two concepts, two failure vocabularies — that alone justifies two names.

The coordination point you mention then lands naturally on the journal spine: a thread is identified by the spine regardless of which continuity mechanism carries it, and the turn record simply says which one — and per your naming instinct, the profile declares which concept its client speaks rather than pretending one subsumes the other. It also has a nice consequence for the frontier clients: since the backend journals everything anyway, reconstruction is available as a _fallback_ for them — a dangling session pointer degrades to a rebuilt-context continuation instead of a dead thread. The reverse is never true for gguf seats, which is the asymmetry that confirms these are two concepts and not one with grades: one mechanism can substitute for the other, but only in one direction.

The design landing spot is that workspace anchoring belongs in the seat's profile as a first-class field — the workspace root plus what's expected to resolve from it — and the exchange tool guarantees launch-from-root by construction, replicating exactly what you do manually when you cd before launching. That also upgrades the probe discipline from L1: reachability ("is the binary there, does it auth") isn't enough; the preflight should be a _provisioning_ probe — does the seat actually see the settings, permissions, and MCP servers its profile says it should. The Antigravity skill's cheap "reply OK" probe checks the first tier; a "list your MCP servers / confirm tool X is available" probe checks the second, and it's the second that would have caught this class of friction before a $5 engagement ran degraded. It slots cleanly into the shared-MCPs coordination story too — the config declares what both sides should mount, and the provisioning probe is how the backend verifies the declaration actually took effect on the worker side rather than trusting the projection worked.
