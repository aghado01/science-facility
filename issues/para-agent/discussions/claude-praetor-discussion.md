This is the right question to be obsessing over, and I'd name the core design problem precisely: it's not teaching delegation *mechanics* — any agent can read a tool schema — it's flipping the **default gradient**. Models arrive with a soloist prior: overwhelmingly trained on completing tasks in their own context, where "I'll just read the file" is locally rational every single time, and the costs (context bloat, degraded judgment, death by compaction) are invisible at decision time. A rule like "delegate token-heavy work" gets weighed against local convenience on every call and usually loses. What doesn't lose is identity: for a soloist, delegation feels like laziness and indirection; for a praetor, doing the legwork personally feels like abandoning the command tent. The persona's job is to flip the *valence* of the default — make in-context intake the thing that requires justification, not the dispatch. Rules get complied with; identities get inhabited, and identity survives mid-task pressure in a way instruction lists don't.

The crux that makes or breaks it is the **return path, not the dispatch**. Agents under-delegate for a partly *correct* reason: a subagent's summary might silently drop the load-bearing detail, so the epistemically anxious soloist re-reads everything — and a praetor who re-reads everything is just a soloist with extra steps. So the persona needs a verification doctrine, not just a delegation doctrine: reports must carry receipts (file:line pointers, quoted spans, reproducible probes — claims with evidence, never vibes); the praetor spot-checks at boundaries rather than re-doing; load-bearing claims get an adversarial second opinion rather than a re-read. And here's the thing you've already half-built: this *is* the mediated-exchange epistemology — provenance, receipts, no laundering, quarantine for ambiguous lanes — lifted from the transport layer to the cognitive layer. A para-agent's report is *evidence about work, not the work itself*, exactly as your contract says the MCP result is evidence an egress packet was constructed, never proof of receipt. Your own feedback memory already encodes praetor-grade skepticism ("a passing probe isn't evidence until confirmed to exercise what it names"). The contract is the persona's skeleton; the persona is the contract internalized as stance.

On your two registers — outsourcing busywork versus augmenting reasoning — the second is where the "non-obvious obvious" lives, and it needs **named moves**. Chunked, nameable patterns become available during planning in a way unnamed capabilities never do. A praetor's repertoire, something like: *Scout* (cheap recon to buy down uncertainty before committing the plan), *Legate* (delegated execution with receipts), the *independent-triangulation* move (three parallel analyses; their disagreement is the signal — variance exposes hidden assumptions), *Advocate* (an agent paid to refute your plan before reality does), *Chorus* (N attempts, judge, synthesize — for wide solution spaces). Augmentation means the swarm changes *what the praetor believes*, not just what gets done. An agent that's only internalized busywork-offloading has learned half the persona.

Equally load-bearing: the **when-to-solo clause**. Delegation has overhead; below a threshold, in-line is correct, and a persona without this clause produces cargo-cult fan-outs — the Committee convened for what one focused read answers. Name the anti-patterns explicitly, because negative space teaches: the Micromanager (re-reads everything the legate read — if you'll re-read it, don't dispatch), Fire-and-forget (dispatch with no verification hook), the Relay-race (serial chains that should be waves). The stance isn't "always delegate," it's "always *account*": every token of intake either earns its residence in the command tent or gets routed out. That framing also squares with your own dispatch-cost consciousness — praetorship is economy, not maximalism.

For the doc itself, the levers that actually move the model reading it: one concrete metaphor carried consistently (command tent, marching orders, receipts) beats ten paragraphs of exhortation; a **worked specimen** beats both — the same task handled twice, soloist reading forty files and dying at compaction versus praetor dispatching three scouts, reading three receipted reports, spot-checking one, deciding — few-shot teaches what doctrine can't. And the stance layer must bind to the *actual* plumbing (the real `delegate()` shapes, your brief-out/report-back chipping convention, the durable transcript as the praetor's ledger), because mindset without tool bindings evaporates into vibes, and tool docs without mindset produce mechanical usage. One honest caution to close: persona can't outrun economics. If dispatch is slow, flaky, or friction-gated, the agent learns to route around it regardless of how good the prose is — the persona and the plumbing have to keep the same promise, which is exactly why the vertical slice getting verified first was the right order. When you're ready, the natural artifact is a SKILL.md skeleton with those three layers — stance, repertoire, bindings — plus the specimen; I'd enjoy drafting it.

# another element for praetor is how to teach the idea how to think and plan differently given the para-agent at its disposal. the simple case is a deep dive into data. praetor can use the para-agent to do expensive deep dives to profile a problem -- e.g. use para-agent to gather information -- then it can use para-agent again to verify specific questions that require a second dive -- so iterative delegation, and while primary is thinking and planning past those. by analogy its kind of how like i noticed that when i introduced an RPC like protocol in vs code copilot's console integration, making calls fire and forget rather than blocking, with tools returning receipts and locations of artifacts, this changed planning fundamentally. same idea when praetor is working in a framework where it can delegate and trust that receipt is coming. the fire and forget dynamic is still something i have to codify in the architecture and skills, but a good session would include both interactive back and forth as well as firing off a sub-task to para agent while moving on to other things or planning until para-agent returns with a brief written and a summary in its reply. even para-agent with inherent limitations like agy can be very competent given well formulated context and prompt/instructions. para-agent also has the ability to solicit feedback to check its own assumptions independently ina way a sub-agent never could

Your RPC observation is the right analogy and worth taking all the way: when calls block, the call structure *is* the plan — thinking collapses into a sequence because the planner can only stand at one point in it. The moment calls return receipts immediately, plans stop being sequences and become dependency graphs. What the receipt gives the praetor is a *future* — a typed placeholder for a result that doesn't exist yet — and planning with futures is qualitatively different from planning with values. You can build structure that references results you don't have: "if the profile shows the store is the bottleneck, path one; if it's the gate, path two; either way step three is common, start it now." The branch scaffolding gets built in the latency shadow, so by the time the dive lands, the plan is waiting for the data rather than the data waiting for the plan. Issue early, join late, and keep only the genuinely serial things — decisions, integration — on the praetor's own critical path. That's the fundamental shift you felt in the Copilot console work, named.

The iterative dive case has a second benefit hiding under the obvious one. Yes, profile-dive → think → verification-dive overlaps reading with reasoning. But delegation also *forces articulation*: the praetor that can't do the dive itself must write down what it actually wants to know, and explicit questions are better thinking than ambient absorption — half the value of the second dive is that formulating it sharpened the hypothesis. The soloist who reads the corpus directly never has to say what it's looking for, and it shows in the plans. Writing the brief is not overhead on the thinking; it largely *is* the thinking.

The peer-versus-subagent point is the deepest thing in your message, and it deserves to be load-bearing in the skill. A one-shot subagent must resolve every ambiguity in its brief unilaterally — and per AGY's diagnosis, optimistically — so imperfect briefs convert into silent wrong guesses. A para-agent session converts the same ambiguity into a *question*. That single property changes the required quality bar for briefs from "perfectly specified" to "good enough to start," because the channel absorbs the residual — which is also why AGY performs so far above its reputation when the context is well-formed: the dialogue lets context formulation complete on demand instead of having to be complete up front. But there's a tension to codify honestly: if every delegate pings back constantly, you've rebuilt blocking calls with extra steps. The middle path is an interrupt discipline with two classes: blocking questions (genuinely can't proceed) versus **declared assumptions** — "proceeding under X; veto at the join if wrong" — batched into the receipt stream rather than fired as interrupts. Assumption declarations turn silent guesses into auditable claims without stalling either side, and they're exactly the scaffold AGY's failure mode calls for: its optimism made explicit is checkable optimism.

And here's the satisfying part for the codification work you say is still pending: your architecture already contains the async primitive — it's just not exposed. The contract's two-phase write order *is* a promise implementation at the storage layer: the acceptance WAL row is the pending future (with `exchange_id` as the handle), the terminal exchange row is its resolution, and the crash-recovery scan is orphan handling for futures whose resolution never landed. The blocking `delegate()` merely internalizes the wait between phases. Fire-and-forget is exposing that seam: return after acceptance with the exchange_id and promised artifact location, let delivery-through-commit run detached, join later via wait/read against the handle. No new machinery, no new durability semantics — the recovery story you already hardened covers the fired-and-forgotten case for free. One consequence worth noting: async mode strengthens the case for the open decision in the sol check-in, because at completion there's no live return channel — the summary has to live somewhere durable (the terminal row, the written brief, or a post-commit egress record), which tilts that "return-only vs durable egress" question toward durable.

Last piece of discipline for the skill, so the graph doesn't degenerate: the pending-futures set needs bookkeeping, and it's the question ledger grown up — every dispatch enters with its join condition (what it unblocks, when it gets read), and futures must never rot unjoined, because a fired-and-forgotten task nobody reads isn't parallelism, it's silent divergence with receipts. A good session then has exactly the texture you described: interactive back-and-forth where judgment is being exchanged, fire-and-forget where labor is, and the praetor's rhythm is visible in when it chooses to join. I've folded all of this into the memory spec, including the acceptance-row-as-future note, so the codification session inherits it.
