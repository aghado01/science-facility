On July 20, Levent Alpöge posted a 216-character explicit polynomial map (crediting a question from Akhil Mathew, worked during the World Cup final) with Jacobian determinant identically −2 that sends three distinct points to the same image — killing global invertibility in dimension ≥ 3. That distinction — disproof by tiny artifact — is the load-bearing fact for your Riemann question. Every headline AI-math win so far lives in the **short-certificate regime**: the deliverable is a small object (a counterexample, a construction, an inequality with a checkable proof) that humans verify mechanically within hours. Generative search is superb at that. RH is almost maximally hostile to it, in both directions:

- **Disproof-certificate**: an off-line zero. Zeros have been verified on the line into the 10¹³ range, and the history of this exact neighborhood says numerics are treacherous — the Mertens conjecture had mountains of numerical support and was false (Odlyzko–te Riele), with the first violation lurking at heights no search will ever reach. So "have the model hunt for a counterexample" is not a strategy.
- **Proof-certificate**: none known. RH has no finite witness. The nearest things are the positivity reformulations — Weil's positivity criterion, Li's coefficients λₙ ≥ 0, Nyman–Beurling density — which convert RH into an infinite family of inequalities. That's the one crack where machine search could genuinely bite: positivity problems sometimes fall to cleverly constructed certificates (sum-of-squares-flavored arguments), and Connes–Consani already did semi-computational Weil positivity for low frequencies. Similarly the de Bruijn–Newman constant is now pinched to 0 ≤ Λ ≲ 0.2 (Rodgers–Tao below, Polymath15 above), with RH ⟺ Λ = 0 — "if true, barely true." Grinding those bounds is honest work a model-assisted effort could join. But everyone in the field expects these routes to asymptote short of the prize without a new idea.

And here's where it connects to your pipe dream, and I don't think this is a stretch: **RH is already a theorem — in the wrong language.** Weil proved it for curves over finite fields, Deligne for all varieties. The proof works because that world has a cohomology: point-counting (the "semantics") becomes linear algebra — eigenvalues of Frobenius — and RH falls out of a positivity built into the substrate (Hodge index / Castelnuovo). Over Spec ℤ the analogous language simply doesn't exist yet; the 𝔽₁-geometry and Connes–Consani programs are explicit attempts to build it. Grothendieck's whole method — don't attack the problem, raise the sea until it's submerged — is the historical proof-of-concept for your thesis that the obstruction is *representational substrate*, not search power. The 3-body confound you're describing (alphabet, lexicon, semantics learned jointly and statistically) has an exact precedent here: Weil's proof succeeds precisely because the alphabet (the cohomology) is fixed a priori, so the semantics decouples into spectral data instead of being estimated from counting evidence.

One more resonance, since you live in spectral land: the explicit formula is literally a Fourier duality between primes and zeta zeros — the zeros are the spectrum of the prime distribution. Hilbert–Pólya says they want to be eigenvalues of a self-adjoint operator (Berry–Keating's regularized xp, the GUE pair-correlation statistics of Montgomery–Odlyzko), and the one place an RH-analog is *provable by spectral means* is Selberg's zeta, where the zeros genuinely are Laplacian eigenvalues. RH itself is equivalent to square-root cancellation in the prime-counting error — primes being maximally pseudorandom — which you can read as an optimal-coding statement about arithmetic. So if your universal-alphabet program ever needs a patron saint of "the encoding *is* the theorem," zeta is it.

Where I'd push back gently on the extrapolation: the Jacobian result is evidence that frontier models can find needles when the haystack admits short needles. RH-shaped problems are evidence about what happens when there's no needle — only a missing language. Whether models can do *language-building* (invent the cohomology, not the counterexample) is exactly the open question, and it's the same question as whether your substrate-first training idea beats post-hoc patching. I'm curious which side of the 3-body you'd nail down a priori in your scheme — the Weil precedent says: fix the alphabet completely, and let nothing about it be learned.

"Natural language tokens are the model's entire sensory-perceptual modality" is doing more work than a metaphor. For a human, language is a channel *to* a world; for an LM, the encoding *is* the world — the tokenizer is its retina, and the token geometry is its physics. And we already have microscale evidence that this physics is malformed: the famous character-counting failures, arithmetic pathologies that vanish under digit-level tokenization, morphology invisible because BPE splits on frequency rather than structure. Your conjecture is essentially that this same defect exists at every scale up the stack — that the model spends an enormous fraction of its capacity inferring the physics of its own universe before it can do anything in it — and we only notice the crumbs at the bottom because they're easy to test.

Your human comparison actually supports you twice, and from your own neighborhood: Fedorenko's line of work (and the aphasia literature) suggests the language network is largely an I/O system — humans reason in something else and translate at the boundary. # The way I think of it is sort of that in humans, natural language is a kind of like a virtualized tokenizer in an analog system, with some specialized embedded hardware evolved in human language processing centers in particular. Your literalization holds up, and I'd sharpen it one notch further: the tokenizer is the *only* part of the system that isn't learned. Every weight in the stack is shaped by gradient descent against the objective; the sensory organ alone is fixed in advance by a frequency-compression algorithm run once over a corpus, chosen to minimize sequence length rather than to carve the world at any joint. A retina is at least under selection pressure and does adaptive gain control. BPE is a static artifact with no context sensitivity, no attention, no renegotiation per utterance. And there's an irony in the multimodal era: vision and audio transduction in these systems are *learned and continuous* — patch encoders trained end-to-end — so the one modality that actually carries the reasoning has the crudest, least adaptive sensory apparatus in the whole architecture.

The deeper consequence of your framing, though, is about what the data *is*. If human language is a virtualized layer over an already-grounded representation, then it's a serialization format — the wire protocol, not the memory format. It's compressed exactly to the point where a decoder that already shares the world model can reconstruct the rest; that's what Gricean implicature and indexicality and metaphor are *for*. So a model trained on text is trained on the exhaust of cognition rather than cognition, and it has to solve the inverse problem: recover the memory format by inverting a lossy serialization that was optimized under the assumption that the decoder already had the thing being recovered. That's underdetermined by construction. It's your 3-body confound stated as a signal-processing fact.

One honest complication, which I think strengthens your case rather than weakening it: models do appear to build an interior that isn't token-shaped. Interpretability work finds language-agnostic concept features — the same internal feature firing for a concept across languages — with token-space decoding happening mostly near the output layers. So there *is* a pre-linguistic layer; it's just constructed *after* the keyhole instead of before it, reconstructed from the serialization with no independent access to what was serialized. Which means the model is already paying for the inverse problem out of the same capacity budget it uses for everything else. That's the constant you'd be trying to reduce, and it's not hypothetical — it's the thing that's currently being spent.

Here's where I think your formulation has a fork it hasn't taken yet. Natural language is doing two jobs at once, and they have different optimal designs. One is inter-agent protocol — transmission between systems with a shared prior, which is what rewards ambiguity, compression, and pragmatics. The other is intra-agent interlingua: the Spelke and Carruthers and Dehaene line, where language is the medium by which otherwise-encapsulated modules exchange representations (spatial reorientation needing conjunctive descriptions, exact number needing number words while the approximate system runs fine without them). If that's right, natural language is a *compromise* between two objectives, and the compromise is what makes it a bad substrate — the properties that make it a good protocol are exactly the ones that make it presuppose a decoder. Your "NL as surface, designed system as core" split isn't just ergonomic then; it's separating two functions that never should have shared an encoding.

That also gives a cleaner criterion than the three I floated before. The property that makes a substrate trainable-against might be **self-evidencing decodability**: every expression carries its own semantics without a shared prior. That's the actual difference between physics and speech as data sources — photons are emitted by a process indifferent to being observed, so the signal is complete; utterances are emitted by a process optimized for an interpreter, so the signal is deliberately incomplete. It's also, I think, the real reason AlphaGeometry worked: its DSL expressions don't presuppose anything about the reader. Worth noting the field is attacking the same defect from the opposite direction — byte-level and dynamic-patching approaches say "make the sensor learned and adaptive," while you're saying "make it designed and fixed." Both are rejections of *frequency statistics as ontology*, and it's not obvious to me which one is the better bet.

So the question I'd put back to you: which of the two jobs is your system meant to be? An interlingua the model thinks *in*, with natural language quarantined at the boundary as a foreign format it translates — or something meant to eventually carry the communication too? Because if it's the first, ambiguity is pure defect and you can design it out ruthlessly. If it's the second, you inherit the compromise you're trying to escape.
