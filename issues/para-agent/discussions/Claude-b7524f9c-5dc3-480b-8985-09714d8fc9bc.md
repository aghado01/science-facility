# i find myself wondering about getting creative with security. surely i can encrypt the channels and artifacts with a secret. but what about secret water marks hidden in mediation layer, two of them such that they form a sort of invisible handshake, so that if one agent was compromised or spoofed in adversarial attack, it could be detected

The instinct is good, but it's worth splitting your threat into its two halves first, because they need different machinery. **Spoofing** (wrong process talking on the lane) is defeated by cryptographic authenticity — and note that's MACs/signatures, not encryption; an encrypted-but-unauthenticated channel still accepts a forger who has the socket. **Compromise** (the right process, subverted behavior — prompt injection being the realistic case) is the nastier one, because a compromised agent authenticates *perfectly*: it still holds its keys and signs everything correctly. No transport secret detects it. So your two watermarks are actually pointing at two different detectors, and I'd lean into that rather than making them two halves of one mechanism.

For the spoofing half, the rigorous version of your invisible handshake is challenge-response with **split keys**. Mediator stamps ingress with a per-exchange nonce; the egress must carry a binding over (nonce ‖ payload hash ‖ exchange ID ‖ store generation). The reason to have *two* marks isn't stealth — it's that the two halves should be computed in **different trust domains**: one keyed by the mediator core, one by the adapter at raw-capture time, verified together at the assembler→commit point. Then a single compromised component can't forge the whole handshake, and a transplanted-but-valid mark on different content fails the payload binding. Binding to store generation kills cross-generation replay, which you get almost free since generations already exist. And you already own the perfect enforcement path: a failed handshake is precisely an "ambiguous commit" — route it to the existing quarantine lane.

Here's the pitfall that kills naive versions of this: **anything that transits the model's context is disclosed to whatever compromised the model.** A prompt injector reads the context; if your secret token rides in the prompt so the agent can echo it back, the injector sees it and complies while doing its damage elsewhere. So handshake secrets must live strictly out-of-band — in adapter/mediator process memory, never in model-visible text. Your architecture is actually well-shaped for this, since the mediation layer wraps native clients whose text plane you already treat as untrusted evidence.

In-band marks still have a job, but it's the *other* detector: **canaries** for behavioral compromise. Plant tokens in context whose significance is unknown — strings a well-behaved agent would never echo or exfiltrate, or a must-preserve marker whose absence in egress means something rewrote the pipeline. These are tripwires, not proofs: they catch sloppy and generic attacks, and a targeted attacker who's read your context can step over them. That's fine — they're detection-in-depth on top of the crypto floor, not the floor.

On the "invisible" part, be honest with yourself about what stealth buys. Unforgeability must come from keys, not concealment (Kerckhoffs — assume the scheme is known, only the keys secret), so design it to survive discovery. What stealth legitimately buys is your detection surface: when a handshake fails, *don't tell the sender*. Fail silent, quarantine, alert you. A tripwire that stays invisible on trigger is worth far more than one that teaches the attacker where the wires are.

Two cheap adjacent wins while you're in this territory: derive per-session ephemeral keys at spawn time and hand them to the adapter via stdin/env — a spoofed process that didn't come from your spawner never possesses a key at all, and replay dies with the session. And for the durable store, given your no-laundering posture, tamper-*evidence* probably beats secrecy: hash-chain the committed transcripts (each commit includes the previous digest) so any retroactive edit breaks the chain. That, plus the handshake as an admission test, also gives you a principled gate for AGY when you eventually capture its stream: no adapter joins the pool until it can complete the handshake.

# well anthropic also just publicly disclosed watermarking in all models. presumably in tokens generated by models, maybe in some kind of whitespace shenanigans. unknown and im not really asking. but in theory if the primary agent of some platform has its own native finger print, can this be in theory used as some kind of security buff?

Taking the premise as theoretical (I can't speak to what Anthropic actually deploys, and the published schemes in the literature — green-list logit biasing, PRF-seeded sampling, SynthID-style approaches — all work at the token-sampling level, which is worth knowing because it shapes what the signal can and can't do): yes, it's a security buff, but of a very specific and weak kind, and the failure mode is trusting it for the thing it structurally cannot do.

The core limitation: a model-native watermark authenticates the **species, not the individual**. Every instance of the model everywhere carries the same fingerprint. So it answers "were these tokens sampled from model X?" — never "were they sampled by *my* session, in response to *my* delegated task?" An attacker spoofing your agent doesn't need to defeat the watermark; they just use the same model to generate the spoofed traffic, and the mark validates their forgery as happily as your real exchange. There's no session binding, no principal, no key you hold, no revocation. In certificate terms it's a CA-signed cert with no subject name, where only the CA can run verification. And for your nastier threat half it's actively misleading: a prompt-injected agent's output is genuinely model-generated, so the watermark stays pristine through the entire compromise. If anything it manufactures false confidence there.

Where it *does* have real value in your architecture is the invariant you already care most about: **laundering detection**. Your contract says live-stream only, no model-fallback substitution. A native fingerprint is exactly an evidence-plane check on that — did this adapter lane actually invoke the claimed model, or did something swap in a cheaper one, or fabricate transcripts without spending tokens at all (a stub left in prod produces unwatermarked text). And the statistical nature of these schemes, which makes them useless for per-message gating — you need tens-to-hundreds of tokens for detection power, and a one-line egress proves nothing — becomes a non-problem if you run it as a **retrospective audit over the durable store**: aggregate detection across a lane's accumulated exchanges, where the statistics get arbitrarily strong. Audit tolerates detection lag; gates don't. That's the slot: lane-level anomaly audit, not commit-gate check. One architectural note if this ever becomes real: run it over the raw-capture plane, not assembled transcripts — token-level and especially whitespace-borne signals are exactly what your own assembler, serializers, and normalization would shred, and your byte-checked raw plane is the only place the signal survives intact.

Two caveats that keep it honest. Absence of the mark is uninformative — benign transformation, truncation, or paraphrase washes it out, so "no watermark" can't be treated as "spoofed," which makes it a tripwire that only fires in one direction. And detection requires the provider's key, so unless a detection API exists, the whole thing is an attestation you consume on the provider's terms, not a control you own — an external dependency sitting inside your security posture.

The theoretical upgrade that would make it a genuine primitive is **keyed, per-tenant watermarking**: if a provider let you say "watermark my org's generations under my key and hand me the detector," the species/individual problem collapses — your instances become distinguishable from an attacker's instances of the same model, and you hold verification locally. That's the shape it would need to take to graduate from evidence to authentication. Even then, note, the compromise case stays uncovered — an injected agent still generates under your tenant key — which is the recurring lesson of this whole thread: generation-side provenance, no matter how strong, authenticates origin, never intent. Your handshake and canaries still carry that load; the fingerprint would just make the text plane itself testify about where it came from.

Yes — that's the correct tail of the pipeline, matching steps 4–8 of the contract's physical write order ([MEDIATED-EXCHANGE-CONTRACT.md:117](mcp/para-agent/contract/MEDIATED-EXCHANGE-CONTRACT.md)). Three precision notes:

1. **It's the back half, not the whole flow.** Durable acceptance comes *before* any of it: validate + gate → writer lease → acceptance WAL row assigning `exchange_id` → prompt delivery. Only then does native-event collection begin. That ordering is what makes crash recovery sound — an acceptance row with no terminal marker is exactly what the restart scan reconciles.

2. **Your `+` hides a load-bearing order.** It's terminal exchange row first (assigning `exchange_index` in the writer lane), *then* the WAL terminal marker — never marker-first. Recovery depends on that asymmetry: crash between the two → terminal exchange exists, marker missing → append the missing marker; crash before the exchange row → append an `interrupted` exchange, then mark. Marker-first would let recovery see a "terminalized" exchange that was never written.

3. **"Obtain final receiver reply" is the `completed` lane only.** The reply isn't a separate fetch after collection — it's the turn-correlated native terminal event arriving *within* the correlated stream (screen stability and process silence don't count). For `failed` / `interrupted` / `timeout`, the same commit tail runs — terminal exchange + marker — but with no fabricated reply, and step 5 constructs an MCP *error* carrying the durable receipt instead of a result.

Also worth keeping in mind as you phrase it: "construct and return" is deliberately post-commit and return-only — egress construction is a separate stage that's evidence para-agent built a packet, never evidence the primary received it. Whether it gains its own durable post-commit record is the open decision from the check-in.

This is the right question to be obsessing over, and I'd name the core design problem precisely: it's not teaching delegation *mechanics* — any agent can read a tool schema — it's flipping the **default gradient**. Models arrive with a soloist prior: overwhelmingly trained on completing tasks in their own context, where "I'll just read the file" is locally rational every single time, and the costs (context bloat, degraded judgment, death by compaction) are invisible at decision time. A rule like "delegate token-heavy work" gets weighed against local convenience on every call and usually loses. What doesn't lose is identity: for a soloist, delegation feels like laziness and indirection; for a praetor, doing the legwork personally feels like abandoning the command tent. The persona's job is to flip the *valence* of the default — make in-context intake the thing that requires justification, not the dispatch. Rules get complied with; identities get inhabited, and identity survives mid-task pressure in a way instruction lists don't.

The crux that makes or breaks it is the **return path, not the dispatch**. Agents under-delegate for a partly *correct* reason: a subagent's summary might silently drop the load-bearing detail, so the epistemically anxious soloist re-reads everything — and a praetor who re-reads everything is just a soloist with extra steps. Your own feedback memory already encodes praetor-grade skepticism ("a passing probe isn't evidence until confirmed to exercise what it names"). The contract is the persona's skeleton; the persona is the contract internalized as stance.

On your two registers — outsourcing busywork versus augmenting reasoning — the second is where the "non-obvious obvious" lives, and it needs **named moves**. Chunked, nameable patterns become available during planning in a way unnamed capabilities never do. Equally load-bearing: the **when-to-solo clause**.
